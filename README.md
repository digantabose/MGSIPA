## 1. About this documentation

This repository documents the content, evidence, demonstrations,
governance framework and practical recommendations developed for the
MGSIPA training session held on 9 September 2026.

The session was designed for education leaders and public-sector
education stakeholders rather than for a purely technical audience. Its
purpose was to examine how generative AI can be used in education while
preserving accuracy, academic integrity, privacy, fairness, human
judgment and institutional accountability.

The presentation was deliberately structured around a simple sequence:

> **Capability → Risk → Governance**

The central question was therefore not whether educational institutions
should use AI. AI use is already occurring among teachers,
administrators and students. The more useful question is:

> **Under what conditions should AI be used, what risks must be
> controlled, and who remains accountable for the result?**

This documentation expands the presentation into a reusable reference
for educators, principals, administrators, policy teams and institutions
that want to develop responsible AI practices.

## 2. Executive summary

Generative AI can support education through lesson planning,
brainstorming, translation, summarisation, feedback, content adaptation,
research assistance and administrative workflows. However, successful
task completion by an AI system does not automatically mean that
learning has occurred, that the output is accurate, or that the use is
institutionally appropriate.

The OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026 reports that generative AI can
support learning when it is guided by clear pedagogical principles. It
also warns that using general-purpose GenAI without pedagogical guidance
can improve task performance without producing corresponding learning
gains. The report therefore emphasises pedagogical purpose,
human-centred teaching, trustworthy policy, infrastructure and
professional learning. [OECD, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1787/062a7394-en)

At the same time, adoption is already substantial. The Digital Education
Council's 2024 Global AI Student Survey reported that 86% of surveyed
students across 16 countries used AI in their studies, while 58% said
they lacked sufficient AI knowledge and skills. The survey included more
than 3,800 students and should therefore be interpreted as a global
higher-education survey, not as a measurement of all Indian students.
[Digital Education Council,
2024](https://www.digitaleducationcouncil.com/newsroom/what-students-want-key-results-from-dec-global-ai-student-survey-2024)

The governance problem is therefore two-sided:

1.  Institutions need to understand where AI can create educational
    value.
2.  Institutions need controls for cases where AI can create
    unacceptable risk.

The presentation organised these risks into four practical lenses:

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Lens                                Core question
  ----------------------------------- -----------------------------------
  Accuracy                            Can we rely on the output?

  Ethics                              Is the use fair, appropriate and
                                      respectful of rights?

  Integrity                           Does the use preserve intellectual
                                      honesty and the learning objective?

  Governance                          Who establishes the rules, verifies
                                      the output and remains accountable?
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

The most important principle emerging from the session is:

> **AI output is an input to human decision-making, not a substitute for
> human accountability.**

# 3. Why this topic matters

Education systems are experiencing a rapid change in how information,
writing, assessment and administrative work are produced.

A teacher can ask an AI system to generate a lesson plan. A student can
ask it to explain a concept. An administrator can use it to draft a
circular. A researcher can use it to organise literature. A principal
can use it to summarise a report.

The same system can therefore operate in contexts with very different
consequences.

A wrong sentence in a brainstorming exercise may be harmless.

A wrong sentence in a government circular, examination instruction,
disciplinary order or student record can have substantially greater
consequences.

This leads to an important governance principle:

> **Governance requirements should increase with the consequence,
> sensitivity and scale of an AI-assisted activity.**

The OECD's 2026 education outlook similarly distinguishes useful
educational applications from uses that may outsource cognitive work
without producing learning. [OECD,
2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-digital-education-outlook-2026_062a7394-en.html)

# 4. Indian education context

AI readiness cannot be separated from the existing digital
infrastructure of schools and institutions.

The UDISE+ 2024-25 data show that India had approximately 1.47 million
schools. The dataset reports 852,684 schools with functional computer
facilities for pedagogical purposes, equivalent to about 58% of schools.
It reports 933,987 schools with internet facilities, approximately 63.5%
of schools. Functional electricity was reported for 91.9% of schools.
[Ministry of Education, UDISE+
2024-25](https://dashboard.udiseplus.gov.in/report-new-v.6-demo2025/static/media/UDISE%2B2024_25_Booklet_existing.118ba29d4773e6372f72.pdf)

These figures matter because AI readiness is more than access to a
chatbot. It also depends on:

-   electricity
-   devices
-   connectivity
-   digital literacy
-   teacher capacity
-   institutional policy
-   privacy and security controls
-   assessment design
-   technical support
-   procurement and vendor management

The CBSE has also moved toward explicit AI readiness in school
education. For the 2026-27 session, CBSE introduced a Computational
Thinking and Artificial Intelligence curriculum for Classes III-VIII,
with an emphasis on computational thinking, digital literacy,
responsible technology use, critical thinking and ethical
decision-making. [CBSE, 2026](https://cbseacademic.nic.in/ct-ai.html)

This makes responsible AI literacy a practical education-policy issue
rather than a purely technological topic.

# 5. The central framework: Capability → Risk → Governance

## 5.1 Capability

Generative AI systems can:

-   generate text
-   summarise information
-   translate content
-   explain concepts
-   brainstorm ideas
-   produce examples
-   provide feedback
-   restructure documents
-   assist with coding
-   support administrative drafting
-   help create teaching materials

These capabilities can reduce routine workload and create new
instructional possibilities.

## 5.2 Risk

The same capabilities can introduce:

-   factual errors
-   fabricated citations
-   misleading confidence
-   bias
-   privacy exposure
-   inappropriate disclosure
-   academic misconduct
-   over-reliance
-   weakened learning
-   unfair automated decisions
-   accessibility problems
-   synthetic media abuse
-   unclear accountability

## 5.3 Governance

Governance determines the conditions under which a capability becomes
acceptable.

A governance process should ask:

1.  What is the purpose?
2.  Is AI necessary for the purpose?
3.  What information will be entered?
4.  How sensitive is the information?
5.  How reliable must the output be?
6.  Who verifies the result?
7.  Who makes the final decision?
8.  How is AI use disclosed?
9.  What happens if the system fails?
10. Who handles complaints or incidents?

# 6. Challenge 1: Hallucinations and administrative liability

## 6.1 What is a hallucination?

In this context, an AI hallucination is an output that contains
unsupported, inaccurate or fabricated information while presenting it in
a plausible form.

The danger is often not obviously absurd output.

The more difficult problem is a response that:

-   sounds professional
-   contains detailed explanations
-   uses confident language
-   contains references
-   appears internally consistent
-   is nevertheless wrong

This is particularly important in administrative and policy contexts.

An AI-generated draft should therefore be treated differently depending
on its purpose.

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Use                     Risk level              Appropriate control
  ----------------------- ----------------------- -----------------------
  Brainstorming lesson    Lower                   Basic review
  topics                                          

  Drafting a generic      Lower                   Teacher review
  outline                                         

  Summarising a verified  Moderate                Compare against source
  document                                        

  Translating a           Moderate to high        Human verification
  government circular                             

  Drafting a legal or     High                    Primary-source
  regulatory                                      verification and
  communication                                   authorised review

  Making a disciplinary   Very high               AI should not be the
  decision                                        sole decision-maker
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

# 7. Case study: Mata v. Avianca

One of the best-known examples of AI-assisted factual failure is *Mata
v. Avianca, Inc.*, a 2023 United States federal court case.

Attorneys submitted a filing containing cases that did not exist. The
court's sanctions order describes the use of ChatGPT and the resulting
fabricated authorities. The court imposed a \$5,000 penalty jointly and
severally on the respondents. [United States District Court for the
Southern District of New York,
2023](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/new-york/nysdce/1%3A2022cv01461/575368/54/)

The educational lesson is broader than the legal profession:

> **A polished AI output is not evidence that the underlying claim is
> true.**

For education administrators, the same principle applies to:

-   government orders
-   court judgments
-   policy references
-   circular numbers
-   examination regulations
-   scholarship rules
-   accreditation requirements
-   institutional procedures

# 8. Citation fabrication

The presentation used citation fabrication as a demonstration of why
AI-generated references require verification.

A particularly useful empirical study is Walters and Wilder (2023),
published in *Scientific Reports*.

The researchers generated 84 short literature reviews using GPT-3.5 and
GPT-4 and evaluated 636 cited works. In their sample, 55% of GPT-3.5
citations were fabricated, compared with 18% for GPT-4. They also found
substantive errors in many citations that corresponded to real works.
These results describe the models and prompts tested in that study and
should not be treated as a universal error rate for all current AI
systems. [Walters & Wilder,
2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-41032-5)

This distinction is important.

The correct conclusion is:

> **AI-generated citations must be independently verified.**

The incorrect conclusion would be:

> **All AI systems fabricate citations at a fixed percentage.**

The first statement is a governance rule. The second is an unsupported
generalisation.

# 9. A practical verification protocol

Before using AI-generated material in an official or consequential
context, use a three-stage verification process.

## Stage 1: Source authentication

Ask:

-   Does the cited document exist?
-   Is it from the claimed institution?
-   Is the URL genuine?
-   Is the document current?
-   Is the cited version authoritative?

For law and policy, navigate directly to the relevant official
repository instead of trusting the URL supplied by an AI system.

## Stage 2: Contextual accuracy

Ask:

-   Does the source actually support the claim?
-   Has the AI omitted an important condition?
-   Is the provision still in force?
-   Does the rule apply to this institution?
-   Has the AI confused two similar regulations?

## Stage 3: Human judgment

Ask:

-   Is this output appropriate for the decision?
-   What is the consequence if it is wrong?
-   Does the decision require a qualified human reviewer?
-   Is there an appeal or correction process?

The higher the consequence, the stronger the verification requirement
should be.

# 10. Challenge 2: Academic integrity and learning

Generative AI creates a more complicated academic-integrity problem than
simply asking whether students used AI.

The same tool can support learning or replace the learning activity.

For example:

### Potentially supportive use

-   brainstorming
-   asking for explanations
-   generating practice questions
-   requesting feedback
-   exploring alternative examples
-   translation support

### Context-dependent use

-   editing
-   translation
-   coding assistance
-   drafting
-   summarisation
-   research organisation

### Potentially undermining use

-   submitting generated work as one's own when independent work is the
    learning objective
-   using AI to complete an assessment without permission
-   generating fabricated references or evidence
-   using AI to answer an examination where independent performance is
    required

The key question therefore becomes:

> **How was AI used, and did that use undermine the learning
> objective?**

# 11. Assessment redesign

AI creates pressure on assessment models that rely heavily on
unsupervised production of polished text.

A stronger assessment strategy can combine:

-   drafts
-   revision history
-   oral explanation
-   process-based assessment
-   source verification
-   classroom work
-   demonstrations
-   reflection
-   viva voce
-   practical tasks
-   AI-use declarations

The purpose is not to make assessment impossible to automate. The
purpose is to collect stronger evidence of learning.

The OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026 is particularly relevant here.
It reports that GenAI can improve task performance without necessarily
improving learning when students outsource cognitive work. It recommends
pedagogically intentional use rather than unrestricted substitution of
student effort. [OECD,
2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-digital-education-outlook-2026_062a7394-en.html)

# 12. AI detectors should not become the integrity system

AI detectors can provide signals, but a detector score should not
automatically be treated as proof that a particular student used AI or
violated an assignment rule.

A detector score cannot independently establish:

-   who produced the work
-   how much AI was used
-   whether AI use was permitted
-   whether the work violates the assignment rules
-   whether the detector is reliable for the language, genre and
    population involved

Liang et al. (2023) reported concerns about bias against non-native
English writers in GPT detection systems. Their work is especially
relevant when institutions consider detector scores in high-stakes
academic decisions. [Liang et al.,
2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.patter.2023.100779)

A safer institutional approach is:

> **Use detection, if at all, as one contextual signal. Require human
> review and additional evidence before making a consequential
> decision.**

# 13. Indian academic-integrity context

The UGC's 2018 regulations define academic integrity around intellectual
honesty in proposing, performing and reporting academic work. The
regulations apply to students, faculty, researchers and staff in higher
educational institutions. [UGC,
2018](https://www.ugc.gov.in/regulations/UGC_Regulations_university)

These regulations predate the widespread use of generative AI. They
should therefore not be presented as an AI-specific policy.

Their underlying principle remains relevant:

> **Institutions need to translate intellectual honesty into explicit
> rules for AI-assisted work.**

An institutional AI policy should clarify:

-   permitted AI assistance
-   restricted uses
-   prohibited uses
-   disclosure requirements
-   attribution expectations
-   responsibility for final work
-   assessment-specific rules
-   procedures for suspected misconduct
-   review and appeal mechanisms

# 14. Challenge 3: Privacy and data governance

Educational institutions routinely handle sensitive information.

Examples include:

-   student names
-   contact details
-   attendance
-   grades
-   disciplinary information
-   health information
-   disability-related information
-   family information
-   behavioural observations
-   examination records
-   staff records

The convenience of an AI chatbot can make it tempting to paste such
information into a prompt.

That creates a governance question:

> **Does the AI system actually need this information to perform the
> task?**

Often, it does not.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 15. Data minimisation

Before entering information into an AI system, use the following
sequence:

### 1. Purpose

What exactly am I trying to accomplish?

### 2. Necessity

Does the task require personal data?

### 3. Minimisation

Can identifiers or unnecessary details be removed?

### 4. Approval

Is the AI service approved for this category of institutional
information?

### 5. Handling

What happens to the data after submission?

### 6. Accountability

Who is responsible if the information is mishandled?

This principle should become a routine part of teacher and administrator
training.

# 16. India's DPDP framework

India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 established a legal
framework for processing digital personal data. The Digital Personal
Data Protection Rules, 2025 were subsequently notified by MeitY on 14
November 2025, with phased commencement.

MeitY's 2025-26 annual report states that the Rules establish the
implementation framework for the DPDP Act and provide an 18-month phased
timeline for compliance. [MeitY,
2026](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2026/04/46face7d48c8f6a97030f713ad5fdab4.pdf)

Institutions should therefore avoid simplistic statements such as:

> "Any use of a public AI chatbot is automatically illegal."

The actual compliance position depends on the nature of the data, the
processing purpose, the institution's role, applicable provisions,
contractual arrangements, exemptions, security safeguards and the
current commencement status of the relevant provisions.

For institutional decisions, the Act and Rules should be consulted
directly, together with legal and organisational guidance.

The MeitY explanatory note also addresses children's data, including
provisions concerning educational institutions and specific
circumstances of processing. [MeitY,
2025](https://www.meity.gov.in/writereaddata/files/Explanatory-Note-DPDP-Rules-2025.pdf)

# 17. Practical privacy rule for educators

A simple rule can prevent many avoidable incidents:

> **Do not paste identifiable student or employee information into an AI
> service unless the institution has explicitly approved that workflow
> and the data-handling requirements have been evaluated.**

Instead of:

> "Summarise this student's report: \[full identifiable record\]"

use a synthetic or minimised example when testing a workflow.

For example:

> "Summarise the following anonymised academic progress information into
> three neutral sentences."

Even then, the institution should determine whether the chosen service
is appropriate for the actual workflow.

# 18. Synthetic media, deepfakes and educational institutions

Generative AI also changes the nature of school and college
communication.

Potential issues include:

-   fake teacher announcements
-   synthetic voice messages
-   manipulated images
-   impersonation
-   cyberbullying
-   fabricated screenshots
-   fraudulent notices
-   misleading student content

The governance response should include:

-   trusted official communication channels
-   verification procedures for unusual requests
-   updated disciplinary rules
-   staff awareness training
-   student digital-literacy education
-   escalation procedures for suspected synthetic media

A useful institutional rule is:

> **An unusual digital message should be verified through an independent
> trusted channel before action is taken.**

# 19. Ethics: moving beyond "AI is good" or "AI is bad"

The ethical question is not whether AI is inherently good or bad.

The useful questions are:

-   Who benefits?
-   Who bears the risk?
-   Who can challenge the decision?
-   Who has access?
-   Who may be excluded?
-   What data are being collected?
-   What assumptions are embedded in the system?
-   What happens when the system is wrong?
-   Can a human meaningfully review the decision?

UNESCO's Guidance for Generative AI in Education and Research advocates
a human-centred approach that addresses ethical, safe, equitable and
meaningful use, data privacy and institutional preparedness. [UNESCO,
2023](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research)

# 20. Human agency

UNESCO's AI Competency Framework for Teachers identifies competencies
across five dimensions:

1.  Human-centred mindset
2.  Ethics of AI
3.  AI foundations and applications
4.  AI pedagogy
5.  AI for professional learning

The framework is organised around three progression levels:

-   Acquire
-   Deepen
-   Create

This is useful for professional development because AI literacy is more
than knowing how to write prompts. Teachers also need to understand
limitations, ethics, pedagogy, evaluation and professional
responsibility. [UNESCO,
2024](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ai-competency-framework-teachers)

UNESCO has also published an AI Competency Framework for Students
covering human-centred mindset, ethics, AI techniques and applications,
and AI system design. [UNESCO,
2024](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ai-competency-framework-students)

# 21. Individual experimentation vs institutional deployment

A critical governance distinction is the difference between an
individual using AI for a low-risk task and an institution deploying an
AI system at scale.

## Individual experimentation

Example:

A teacher uses an AI tool to brainstorm five possible lesson activities.

Questions:

-   Is the output accurate?
-   Is the content appropriate?
-   Is sensitive data involved?
-   Will the teacher verify it?

## Institutional deployment

Example:

A college deploys an AI system for student support or assessment.

Additional questions:

-   Who approved the tool?
-   What data does it collect?
-   Where is data stored?
-   What vendor contracts apply?
-   How is accessibility assessed?
-   How is bias tested?
-   How are errors monitored?
-   Who receives complaints?
-   Who can override the system?
-   How are incidents documented?

Governance should scale with consequence and reach.

# 22. What should an institutional AI policy answer?

Every education institution considering formal AI use should be able to
answer at least these questions:

1.  What uses are allowed?
2.  What uses are restricted?
3.  What uses are prohibited?
4.  What data may be entered?
5.  Which tools are approved?
6.  Who verifies AI-generated information?
7.  Where is human approval mandatory?
8.  How should AI use be disclosed?
9.  What evidence is required when misconduct is suspected?
10. Who is accountable for AI-assisted decisions?
11. How are complaints handled?
12. How often is the policy reviewed?

If these questions have no clear answers, AI use will often be governed
informally by individual staff members or students.

# 24. A 90-day institutional roadmap

## Days 1-15: Audit

Conduct an institution-wide review of:

-   AI tools currently being used
-   teaching use cases
-   administrative use cases
-   assessment practices
-   sensitive data flows
-   existing policies
-   staff concerns
-   student concerns

An anonymous staff survey can be useful because informal AI use may
otherwise remain invisible.

## Days 15-45: Draft

Develop an acceptable-use and governance framework covering:

-   approved tools
-   restricted tools or workflows
-   data handling
-   assessment rules
-   disclosure
-   human oversight
-   incident reporting
-   accountability
-   procurement requirements

## Days 45-60: Train

Train teachers and administrators on:

-   AI fundamentals
-   prompting
-   hallucination verification
-   source checking
-   privacy
-   academic integrity
-   assessment redesign
-   responsible use

Training should not be reduced to prompt engineering.

## Days 60-90: Assess

Review:

-   high-risk assessments
-   AI-assisted workflows
-   privacy controls
-   procurement arrangements
-   staff competence
-   student guidance
-   complaint mechanisms
-   monitoring practices

Then revise the policy based on what was learned.

# 25. A seven-question AI safety check

Before adopting a new AI workflow, ask:

### 1. PURPOSE

Why are we using AI?

### 2. NECESSITY

Do we actually need AI for this task?

### 3. DATA

What information will be provided?

### 4. RELIABILITY

How will the output be verified?

### 5. FAIRNESS

Who could be disadvantaged?

### 6. HUMAN OVERSIGHT

Who makes the final decision?

### 7. ACCOUNTABILITY

Who owns the outcome if the system is wrong?

This checklist can be incorporated into an institutional approval form.

# 26. A practical institutional AI policy template

An institution can structure its policy under the following headings:

## A. Purpose

Explain why AI tools are being used and what educational objectives they
support.

## B. Scope

Identify whether the policy applies to:

-   students
-   teachers
-   administrative staff
-   researchers
-   contractors
-   vendors
-   external service providers

## C. Approved uses

List permitted examples.

## D. Restricted uses

Identify uses requiring prior approval.

## E. Prohibited uses

Define unacceptable activities.

## F. Data protection

Specify what data may and may not be entered into external AI services.

## G. Academic integrity

Define permitted AI assistance, disclosure and attribution.

## H. Assessment

Specify rules for different assessment types.

## I. Human oversight

Identify decisions where AI cannot be the sole basis.

## J. Procurement

Require vendors to provide relevant information about:

-   data handling
-   security
-   retention
-   access
-   model behaviour
-   incident management
-   accessibility

## K. Transparency

Explain how AI use is communicated to students, staff and parents where
appropriate.

## L. Complaints and appeals

Provide a mechanism for challenging AI-assisted decisions.

## M. Monitoring

Specify who reviews the policy and how often.

# 27. Procurement questions for AI vendors

Before purchasing an AI-enabled educational product, institutions should
ask:

### Data

-   What data does the system collect?
-   What data is optional?
-   What is retained?
-   Where is it stored?
-   Who can access it?
-   Is customer data used for model training?
-   Can data be deleted?

### Security

-   What security controls are implemented?
-   How are accounts authenticated?
-   How are incidents reported?
-   What happens after a breach?

### AI behaviour

-   What models are used?
-   How are outputs evaluated?
-   What are known limitations?
-   Can the institution configure safety controls?

### Education-specific concerns

-   How is student performance evaluated?
-   Can staff override the system?
-   Can students challenge an AI-generated result?
-   How is accessibility handled?
-   What evidence exists for educational effectiveness?

A vendor's statement that a product is "AI-powered" or "privacy
compliant" should not replace institutional due diligence.

# 28. Teacher guidance

Teachers can use a simple three-level model.

## Green: low-risk support

Examples:

-   brainstorming
-   generating practice questions
-   creating alternative explanations
-   lesson-plan ideas
-   formatting
-   generic examples

Control:

> Review the output before use.

## Amber: context-dependent use

Examples:

-   translation
-   student feedback
-   coding assistance
-   assessment drafting
-   personalised learning materials
-   research assistance

Control:

> Review the output, consider the data involved, and follow
> institutional policy.

## Red: high-risk use

Examples:

-   disciplinary decisions
-   automated student ranking
-   high-stakes assessment decisions
-   processing sensitive student records in unapproved services
-   legal or regulatory decisions

Control:

> Require formal institutional governance and meaningful human
> decision-making.

# 29. Student guidance

Students should be taught responsible AI use rather than simply being
told to avoid AI.

Students should learn to:

-   verify claims
-   check sources
-   identify hallucinations
-   disclose permitted AI assistance
-   protect personal information
-   avoid fabricated references
-   distinguish assistance from authorship
-   understand assignment-specific rules
-   retain drafts and evidence of their process where appropriate

UNESCO's AI Competency Framework for Students provides a useful
international framework for building these capabilities into curriculum.
[UNESCO,
2024](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ai-competency-framework-students)

# 30. Research and higher education guidance

Researchers using generative AI should be particularly careful with:

-   references
-   quotations
-   research data
-   confidential manuscripts
-   peer-review material
-   participant information
-   unpublished findings
-   copyright-sensitive documents
-   statistical interpretations

A practical research rule is:

> **Never treat an AI-generated reference as verified until the original
> source has been located and checked.**

For research workflows, use established scholarly databases, publisher
pages, DOI records and institutional repositories as verification
sources.

The Walters and Wilder study provides a useful empirical demonstration
of why this matters. [Walters & Wilder,
2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-41032-5)

# 31. A broader risk-management framework

Institutions that want a more formal approach can study the NIST AI Risk
Management Framework.

NIST's AI RMF is designed to help organisations incorporate
trustworthiness considerations into the design, development, use and
evaluation of AI systems. Its Generative AI Profile, NIST AI 600-1,
provides additional guidance on risks specific to generative AI. [NIST,
2024](https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence)

The NIST approach is useful for institutions because it moves the
conversation from vague "AI ethics" toward identifiable risks, controls,
evaluation and monitoring.

# 41. Suggested practical activities for future workshops

The original session used demonstrations because participants might not
have individual laptops.

The following activities can be run from a single presenter laptop and
projector.

## Activity 1: Hallucination verification

Show an AI-generated answer containing:

-   a policy claim
-   a citation
-   a URL
-   a numerical statistic

Ask participants:

> Which parts would you verify first?

Then verify each item using authoritative sources.

## Activity 2: Assessment redesign

Give participants a traditional essay assignment.

Ask:

> How would you redesign this assignment if every student had access to
> GenAI?

Possible answers:

-   oral defence
-   draft history
-   reflection
-   source evaluation
-   process evidence
-   in-class component

## Activity 3: Privacy minimisation

Show a fictional student record containing unnecessary identifiers.

Ask participants to remove information that is not necessary for the
task.

## Activity 4: Policy drafting

Ask an AI system to draft a one-page institutional AI policy.

Then ask participants to audit it for:

-   privacy
-   academic integrity
-   human oversight
-   disclosure
-   accessibility
-   accountability
-   complaints

The purpose is to demonstrate that AI can assist with drafting while
still requiring human governance.

# 42. What institutions should avoid

Avoid these approaches:

### "Ban all AI"

This may be difficult to enforce and does not develop responsible AI
literacy.

### "Allow everything"

This transfers institutional risk to individual users without meaningful
controls.

### "Use the detector as the judge"

A detector score is not equivalent to proof of misconduct.

### "Trust the AI because it cites sources"

References can be fabricated or incorrectly represented.

### "Put all institutional data into the chatbot"

Convenience does not establish a legitimate or secure data workflow.

### "Buy an AI product and create the policy later"

Procurement and governance should be connected from the beginning.

### "Train only the technical staff"

AI-related educational decisions are made by teachers, administrators,
principals, students and policy teams. Governance therefore requires
broad institutional literacy.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 43. What responsible AI adoption should look like

A mature institution should gradually move toward:

-   clear AI policy
-   approved-tool inventory
-   risk classification
-   teacher training
-   student AI literacy
-   data minimisation
-   assessment redesign
-   human oversight
-   vendor due diligence
-   incident reporting
-   grievance mechanisms
-   periodic review
-   evidence-based evaluation

The goal is not maximum AI adoption.

The goal is **appropriate AI adoption**.

# 45. Final message

The purpose of responsible AI governance is not to prevent educators
from using technology.

It is to ensure that:

-   AI supports educational objectives
-   students continue to learn
-   teachers retain professional judgment
-   sensitive information is protected
-   AI-generated claims are verified
-   assessment remains meaningful
-   consequential decisions remain accountable
-   institutions understand the systems they deploy

The session's closing principle was:

> **The safest classroom is not necessarily the classroom without AI. It
> is the classroom where people understand exactly how AI is being used,
> what its limitations are, and who remains responsible for the
> outcome.**

# 46. Evidence and accuracy notes

This section is included because the original presentation was a live
training deck and contained some simplified statements intended to
provoke discussion. This GitHub documentation should be treated as the
more carefully qualified reference version.

## 46.1 UDISE+ infrastructure figures

The presentation used approximately 58% for functional computers and 64%
for internet connectivity. These values correspond closely to the UDISE+
2024-25 data when functional computer facilities for pedagogical
purposes and internet facilities are used as the denominators. The
underlying UDISE+ tables should be consulted for exact definitions and
management categories.

## 46.2 Global student AI adoption

The 86% figure comes from the Digital Education Council's 2024 Global AI
Student Survey. It refers to surveyed students across 16 countries, not
all students globally and not specifically Indian institutions.

## 46.3 "Fewer than 15% of Indian institutions"

The presentation included a statement that fewer than 15% of Indian
institutions had updated academic-integrity or privacy policies. This
documentation does not retain that statement as an established statistic
because the cited Digital Education Council survey does not provide that
specific India-wide institutional statistic.

The DEC survey does report that only 5% of surveyed students said they
were fully aware of their university's AI guidelines and felt that those
guidelines were fully comprehensive. That is a different measure and
should not be converted into an institutional policy-adoption statistic.

## 46.4 AI detector statistics

The presentation included a simplified statement about very high
false-positive rates and attributed it broadly to Stanford research. For
this documentation, the stronger and more precise reference is Liang et
al. (2023), which examined detector performance and bias affecting
non-native English writers.

## 46.5 DPDP claims

The presentation used strong language around public LLMs and student
data. The actual legal position is more nuanced. The Act and Rules, the
nature of the processing, the role of the institution, the data
involved, applicable provisions, exemptions, contracts and commencement
status all matter.

This repository therefore uses the principle of data minimisation and
institutional approval rather than claiming that every public-LLM use
automatically constitutes a statutory violation.

## 46.6 "LLMs are predictive text engines"

This phrase is useful as a teaching simplification, but modern AI
systems can incorporate retrieval, tools, browsing, external databases
and other components. The important practical point is that fluent
output is not itself proof of truth.
